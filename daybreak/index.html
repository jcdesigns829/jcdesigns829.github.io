Brand identity case study

DAYBREAK

An everyday coffee brand for students and young professionals, built around one idea: the first good moment of the morning.

  • ProjectFictional brand brief
  • ScopeLogo, palette, pattern, packaging, cups, posters, social
  • RoleBrand and packaging design, JC Designs
  • TaglineYour moment. Your morning.

01 The briefApproachable, energetic, everyday

DAYBREAK is a fictional coffee company. The brief asked for a brand that feels friendly and full of energy, aimed at college students and young professionals who want good coffee without the seriousness of specialty coffee culture.

The product focus is 12 oz bags of whole bean coffee plus cafe cups, so the identity had to work at shelf size, on a cup sleeve, and as a small social avatar.

02 Where it startedA logo that was really an illustration

The first direction was a sun rising behind a coffee cup above a yellow wordmark. It was warm, but it had three problems:

  • Yellow letters on white had almost no contrast, so a thin outline was doing all the work.
  • It mixed four styles: a gradient sun, a hand drawn cup, an outlined wordmark, and a script tagline.
  • It could not shrink. The cup and steam disappeared at small sizes and in one color.

03 Finding the markDay plus Break, D plus B

The name splits into two words, and the two initials share most of a shape. That led to a D and B monogram, and then to the question of where the coffee lives inside it.

The explorations ran from an interlocked outline monogram, to a bean at the crossing, to a bean inside the B, to the breakthrough: using the counter of the letter as the picture. The D holds a coffee bean. The B holds steam and a mug. Read top to bottom, the mark goes from bean to cup.

Each version was tested from 16 px to 192 px in black and reversed. The final rule: the full mark runs at 48 px or larger, and a plain letter version covers favicons and small stamps.

04 Wordmark and lockupsA quiet word next to a loud mark

The original hand lettering was tested beside the new monogram and clashed with it. The letters were tall and narrow while the monogram is wide and geometric. The wordmark moved to a bold rounded sans with softened corners that echo the monogram, so the two read as one piece.

05 ColorYellow is a fill, navy does the reading

The palette keeps the colors of the first logo and gives each one a job. Contrast testing set the rules: navy passes on both yellow and cream at about 9 to 1, while yellow on cream is close to invisible, so yellow never carries text.

06 PatternThe sunrise comes back

The sun from the first logo returned as a supporting graphic. Paired with the bean, it makes a two icon pattern in three colorways. A steam icon was tested and removed, because two icons read more clearly than three.

07 PackagingThree roasts, three colorways

The three pattern colorways map onto a product line: light, medium, and dark roast. The pattern sits in a band on the lower third so the logo has room. A full wrap version was tested and felt busier, so the band stayed.

08 CampaignPosters and social

The campaign speaks to people with early classes and early meetings. The big sunrise gives the brand a hero image, and the product posts reuse the bags and cups directly.

09 What I learnedThe best idea was already in the letters

  • A logo has to survive one color and 24 px before it earns gradients or detail.
  • Testing contrast early turned color from a taste question into a set of rules.
  • Dropping the first logo did not waste it. Its colors became the palette and its sun became the pattern.

Process and tools

The brief and client feedback were simulated with ChatGPT. Concept development, vector construction of the explorations, and flat mockups were produced in collaboration with Claude.
